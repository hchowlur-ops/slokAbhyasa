          Settings

          How a recitation is judged, and how its words are heard. Everything here is remembered in this browser.

          Weights · what the overall score is made of

          The overall score is the weighted mean of the categories that could be judged; the grade follows it (Excellent from 90, Good from 80, Fair from 65, Needs practice below). The weights recommended for Gītā recitation are phonemes 30, vowel length 25, syllables 15, emphasis 10, pitch contour 10, phrasing 5, timing 5. The weights in force are saved with every attempt and session, so changing them later never rewrites an old score.

          Tolerance · acceptable variation

          A category is within tolerance when its score is at least 100 % minus the tolerance.

          These tolerances apply to Self Evaluation and Teach. A quiz always judges on the defaults for the chosen learner, and every attempt records the tolerance it was judged with.

          Nothing is judged within or outside a tolerance: the tiles, report rows and quiz slokas show their scores and grades alone, and a quiz has no "correct" percentage. Attempts and sessions made while this is on record that they were judged without a tolerance; older ones keep theirs.

          Speech recognition

          The language the words are heard in, the model, and whether transcripts are made without being asked. The same settings appear beside every transcript panel. Saved slokas are transcribed in every supported language in the background, so changing the language later shows that transcript at once.

          The chandas of a sloka is read from its text: anuṣṭubh (4 pādas of 8 syllables, 32) or the triṣṭubh family (4 pādas of 11, 44: indravajrā, upendravajrā, upajāti), the Gītā's own list deciding when a sloka's name says which verse it is. The hint names it under the sloka in Self Evaluation and quizzes, with the pattern in its tooltip; a transcript without line breaks is written out at the pāda or half-verse boundaries; and the results count the syllables heard pāda by pāda.

          Why these defaults

          The defaults are tuned for the recitation of Sanskrit ślokas — the Bhagavad Gītā first of all — where correctness lives in the akṣara, not in the voice.

          The words carry 70 %

          Sanskrit is written as it is spoken, so a slip of the tongue is a slip of a letter. Phonemes (30) compares every consonant and vowel of the recitation with the text by the features the Śikṣā treatises classify sounds with — sthāna, the place of articulation (dental t against retroflex ṭ, s/ś/ṣ), and prayatna, the effort (voicing k/g, aspiration k/kh, nasality) — together with visarga and double consonants. A sound one feature off earns half credit, since it is heard but rarely changes the word; a different sound earns none.

          Vowel length (25) stands on its own because it is meaning-bearing in Sanskrit: a short vowel (hrasva, one mātrā) against a long one (dīrgha, two) can change the word, and it is the commonest slip of learners who think in languages where length is not phonemic. Syllables (15) counts akṣaras missing, added or replaced — in a śloka of 32 syllables (anuṣṭubh, eight to a quarter) a missing akṣara also breaks the metre.

          The manner carries 30 %, relative to your own voice

          Gītā recitation prescribes no absolute pitch, unlike Vedic chant with its svaras, so pitch contour (10) judges only the rise and fall after the key difference is removed, and emphasis (10) compares which syllables stand out — loudness, pitch movement — each standardised to the speaker's own range, so a child or a deep voice is on the same scale as the reference. Phrasing (5) looks at where the pauses fall (the pāda and half-verse boundaries); timing (5) at pauses the śloka does not have and material skipped or added, with the pace itself judged only when asked. None of these uses timbre or voice quality: those are the reciter's voice, not the recitation.

          Tolerances

          Phonemes 15 %, vowel length 20 %, syllables 10 % are set so that a correct recitation passes even though the transcript it is read from is approximate (on a real, well-recited take the three scored 97 / 92 / 95), while a wrong akṣara or several length slips do not. A child's allowance (+5 % at 12–15, +10 % at 8–11, +20 % under 8) follows the finding that speech recognisers make several times more errors on children's voices. The manner categories get 60 % (phrasing 50 %) because they are style-dependent and their measures approximate.

          References

          • Śikṣā, the Vedāṅga of phonetics — the classical classification of Sanskrit sounds by sthāna and prayatna that the feature comparison mirrors.
          • learnsanskrit.org — Vowels and Sanskrit prosody (chandas) — hrasva and dīrgha, one and two mātrās; laghu and guru syllables; the 32-syllable śloka.
          • Automatic Speech Recognition for Sanskrit with Transfer Learning (2025), Automatic Speech Recognition in Sanskrit: A New Speech Corpus and Modelling Insights (2021) and Vedavani: A Benchmark Corpus for ASR on Vedic Sanskrit Poetry (2025) — the state of Sanskrit speech recognition, on which the word categories rest, and why its transcripts are treated as approximate.

          The app's own research notes on voice by age and sex and on recording metadata are in reports/ in the project folder.

          Whisper · how the words are heard

          The words of a sloka and of your attempt are written down by OpenAI's Whisper, a speech-recognition model that runs inside your browser. The first time you use a model tier (Fast, Better or Best) it is downloaded from Hugging Face, 75 MB to 750 MB, and kept in the browser's cache; after that transcription needs no internet connection, and no audio ever leaves this computer. Treat the text as a guide rather than a verdict: Whisper has seen very little Sanskrit and recognises chanted or sung words only approximately, so a transcript can carry errors that are its own, not yours. In particular it writes a recited visarga as the breath it hears, so रताः comes back as रतहः or रताहा, and it writes the anusvāra where the text has a nasal (संग for सङ्ग); the comparison reads both spellings as the sounds they are, but other slips in a transcript can still show up in the word scores. Typing a sloka's canonical text in its Details, or correcting its transcript, gives the comparison a reliable reference.

          SlokAbhyasa

          śloka · abhyāsa — verse, practice

          SlokAbhyasa was created to help students, and anyone learning the Bhagavad Gītā, to learn its ślokas by ear and to recite them well. It is for the learner who has a teacher's recording, or a good recitation, and wants to practise against it: listen as often as you like, at any speed; recite it back; and see exactly where the sounds, the vowel lengths, the syllables, the emphasis, the melody and the pauses differed from the original.

          A śloka is learnt once (recorded, or taken from a file) and kept as an ordinary audio file in your own folders, a chapter at a time. Teach takes one śloka at a time; Self Evaluation compares one recitation with several; a Quiz asks for them from memory and keeps every attempt, so that progress can be seen in Reports. The words are heard by a speech-recognition model that runs on this computer, and the comparison is tuned for Sanskrit recitation: the akṣara, not the voice, is what is judged, so a child and an adult, a man and a woman, are measured on the same scale.

          Everything runs on your own computer. Nothing you record is sent anywhere.

          Weights, tolerances and the references behind them are in Settings. The full guide is the README that comes with the app.
